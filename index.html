2024Representative Democracy
2024 UK General Election — Representative Democracy & Low Turnout
The Example
Labour won a large Commons majority in the 2024 general election, but turnout fell to 59.7% — the lowest since 2001. The Electoral Commission noted that negative views of party politics contributed to people choosing not to vote.
Why It's Relevant
Shows the UK remains a representative democracy (citizens choose MPs), but also demonstrates a possible participation crisis. Many citizens are disengaged from formal politics.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The UK remains a representative democracy because citizens choose MPs through general elections. However, the 2024 election shows a weakness in participation, as turnout fell to 59.7%, suggesting many citizens are disengaged from formal politics.
Best For
representative democracyparticipation crisislegitimacylow turnoutdemocratic deficit
2024Voter ID
Elections Act 2022 — Voter ID Requirements
The Example
The 2024 general election was the first where all voters had to show accepted photographic ID at polling stations. The Electoral Commission found approximately 16,000 people were unable to vote in person without accepted ID. Awareness was lower among 18–24-year-olds and ethnic minority communities.
Why It's Relevant
Shows that legal barriers may reduce participation, especially for groups already less likely to vote. The Electoral Commission also found only 0.08% of those who tried to vote were turned away, so the impact was real but limited in numerical terms.
Best For
participation crisisaccess to votingdemocratic rightselectoral reform

Electoral Systems & Referendums

🎯 Examiner-Style Judgement

  • 2024 general election — best for FPTP, disproportionality and strong government
  • Scottish Parliament 2021 — best for AMS
  • Northern Ireland Assembly 2022 — best for STV
  • EU referendum 2016 — best for direct democracy and participation

FPTP is strong for decisive government but weak for proportional representation, while referendums are strong for direct democracy but weak because they can oversimplify complex issues and create division.
A — First Past the Post
2024FPTP
Labour Won Huge Majority on Only 33.7% of the Vote
The Example
In the 2024 UK general election, Labour won a large Commons majority with only 33.7% of the national vote share. The Electoral Reform Society noted smaller parties took over 40% of the vote while Labour and the Conservatives had their lowest combined vote share in the era of universal suffrage.
Why It's Relevant
FPTP can create strong single-party government, but with only 33.7% of the vote, it exaggerates support for the winning party and damages proportional representation.

The Example
Reform UK won a significant share of the national vote but only 5 seats in 2024, showing how FPTP punishes parties with geographically spread support.
Why It's Relevant
Shows FPTP distorts representation. Even with few seats, Reform UK can still pressure the Conservatives by threatening to split the right-wing vote.

Paper 1 — UK Politics

Voting Behaviour & the Media

🎯 1979 vs 1997 Comparison

  • 1979 — Valence issues (Winter of Discontent), class voting still important, Thatcher's decisive leadership
  • 1997 — Class dealignment, New Labour image, Blair vs Major, The Sun switched to Labour
  • 2019 — Red Wall gains, Brexit, partisan dealignment
  • 2024 — Online media overtook TV; class further weakened as predictor
Short-term factors such as leadership image and valence issues have become more important, while class has weakened as a predictor. Age is now one of the strongest dividing lines, especially since 2017.
1979Valence Issues
1979 — Thatcher, Conservatives and the Winter of Discontent
The Example
Conservatives won 43.9% of the vote (339 seats) vs Labour 36.9% (269 seats). Turnout around 76%. The election followed the Winter of Discontent — strikes and economic problems damaged Labour's competence.
Why It's Relevant
Shows the importance of valence issues. Also shows the beginning of class dealignment — some skilled working-class voters moved towards Thatcher, attracted by home ownership and lower taxation.
Best For
valence issuesleadershipclass votingmedia influence
1997Class Dealignment
1997 Labour Landslide — New Labour and Class Dealignment
The Example
Labour won 418 seats — its largest majority since 1945. Blair rebranded Labour as "New Labour", targeting middle-class voters. The Sun switched from supporting Conservatives to backing Blair in March 1997.
Why It's Relevant
Strong example of class dealignment — Labour won by appealing beyond its traditional working-class base. The Sun's switch shows press influence, but Labour was already far ahead in polls, so the media may have reflected rather than caused the shift.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The media reinforced Labour's image as electable and helped shape public perceptions, but Labour's victory was mainly caused by a combination of New Labour's moderation, Blair's leadership and Conservative weakness.
Best For
class dealignmentleadershipvalence votingmedia influenceparty image
2019Region / Brexit
2019 — Conservative "Red Wall" Gains and Brexit
The Example
Johnson's Conservatives won several historically Labour Red Wall seats in the North and Midlands by promising to "Get Brexit Done".
Why It's Relevant
Shows class and partisan dealignment — many traditionally Labour seats switched Conservative due to Brexit, leadership and dissatisfaction with Corbyn.
Best For
class dealignmentregionBrexitpartisan dealignment
1992Media
"It's The Sun Wot Won It" — 1992
The Example
After the 1992 Conservative victory, The Sun claimed credit with its famous headline and aggressive attacks on Neil Kinnock during the campaign.
Why It's Relevant
Key example for media influence, but also its limits — newspapers often support parties already likely to appeal to their readers, so they may follow public opinion rather than create it.
Best For
media influencenewspaperslimits of media power
2017Age
2017 — Age Becomes a Major Dividing Line
The Example
Younger voters were significantly more likely to support Labour under Corbyn; older voters more likely to support the Conservatives under May.
Why It's Relevant
Age has become more important than traditional class in explaining voting behaviour. Brexit, education, region and leadership also matter — age alone does not explain everything.

agevoting behaviourclass dealignment
2024Social Media
Online News Overtook TV in 2024
The Example
Ofcom's 2024 news research found 71% of UK adults used online sources for news vs 70% using TV. Social media was used by 52% of adults for news.

The UK Constitution

🎯 Key Examples to Prioritise

  • Miller v Prime Minister, 2019 — rule of law, judicial power, uncodified uncertainty
  • Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 — flexibility, parliamentary sovereignty
  • Human Rights Act 1998 — rights protection, parliamentary sovereignty

2019Rule of Law
Miller v Prime Minister 2019 — Prorogation Ruled Unlawful
The Example
The Supreme Court ruled Boris Johnson's prorogation of Parliament was unlawful, holding that prorogation would be unlawful if it prevented Parliament from carrying out its constitutional functions without reasonable justification.
Why It's Relevant
Powerful example of the rule of law checking the executive. Also supports the case for a codified constitution by showing uncertainty over the limits of prime ministerial power.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The 2019 prorogation case shows the UK constitution is not purely based on executive dominance; the judiciary can intervene when core constitutional principles such as parliamentary accountability are threatened.
Best For
rule of lawjudicial powerexecutive limitsuncodified constitution
2022Flexibility
Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022 — FTPA Repealed
The Example
The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 was repealed only eleven years after passing, restoring the PM's power to request dissolution. Shows how easily constitutional reforms can be reversed.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The 2022 Act shows the flexibility of the UK constitution because Parliament was able to reverse the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 through ordinary legislation.
Best For
flexibilityparliamentary sovereigntyweak entrenchmentexecutive power
1998Rights Protection
Human Rights Act 1998 — "Bringing Rights Home"
The Example
The HRA made ECHR rights enforceable in UK courts. If legislation conflicts with rights, courts can issue a declaration of incompatibility — but Parliament decides whether to change the law. The Bill of Rights Bill 2022 attempted to repeal the HRA but was scrapped in 2023.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The Human Rights Act strengthens rights protection but does not fully entrench rights because courts cannot strike down Acts of Parliament.
Best For
rights protectionparliamentary sovereigntyjudicial powercodified vs uncodified
2022Devolution
Scottish Independence Referendum Ruling 2022 — Supreme Court
The Example
The UK Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the Scottish Parliament could not legislate for a second independence referendum without Westminster's approval, as the Union is a reserved matter.
Essay-Ready Sentence
The 2022 Supreme Court ruling showed that the Scottish Parliament remains legally limited because it cannot hold an independence referendum without Westminster's approval.
Best For
devolutionparliamentary sovereigntyconstitutional tensionunion
2023Devolution Tension
Section 35 — Gender Recognition Reform Bill Blocked
The Example
In January 2023, the UK Government used section 35 of the Scotland Act 1998 to block the Scottish Parliament's Gender Recognition Reform Bill, citing impacts on UK-wide equalities law.
Why It's Relevant
Shows Westminster retains significant control over devolved legislation when UK-wide law is affected. Supporters saw it as protecting equalities law; critics saw it as undermining devolution.
Best For
devolution tensionWestminster powerreserved matters
2005Separation of Powers
Constitutional Reform Act 2005 — UK Supreme Court Created
The Example
The Act created the UK Supreme Court (opened 2009) and removed the Law Lords from the House of Lords, strengthening the separation between judiciary and legislature.
Why It's Relevant
Strengthened judicial independence and the separation of powers. However, the UK still has a fused executive and legislature because ministers sit in Parliament.
Best For
separation of powersjudicial independenceconstitutional reform

US Constitution

🎯 Key Examples to Prioritise

  • Article V amendment process — entrenchment, rigidity, comparison with UK
  • Dobbs v Jackson, 2022 — Supreme Court power, federalism, rights
  • Trump v United States, 2024 — presidential power, checks and balances

1787Codified & Entrenched
The US Constitution — Codified and Entrenched Under Article V
The Example
Written in 1787, the Constitution sets out the powers of all three branches and protects rights through the Bill of Rights. Article V means amendments require two-thirds of Congress and ratification by three-quarters of states. Only 27 amendments have ever been ratified.
Why It's Relevant
Contrasts with the UK's uncodified, flexible constitution. Entrenchment protects stability but makes reform very difficult, pushing political conflict into the Supreme Court.
Best For
codified constitutionentrenchmentrigidityUS–UK comparison
2022Federalism / Rights
Dobbs v Jackson 2022 — Roe v Wade Overturned
The Example
The Supreme Court upheld Mississippi's 15-week abortion law and overturned Roe v Wade and Planned Parenthood v Casey, returning abortion law largely to individual states.
Why It's Relevant
Shows the Supreme Court's power to reinterpret the Constitution and overturn precedent. Can be argued as judicial restraint (returning to states) or activism (removing 50 years of protection). Also a key federalism example.
Best For
Supreme Court powerfederalismjudicial activism/restraintrights protection
2024Presidential Power
Trump v United States 2024 — Presidential Immunity Defined
The Example
The Supreme Court ruled Presidents have absolute immunity for core constitutional powers, presumptive immunity for official acts, and no immunity for unofficial acts.
Why It's Relevant
Shows the Supreme Court defining presidential accountability. Supporters argue immunity protects decisive action; critics argue it risks placing presidents above ordinary legal accountability.
Best For
presidential powerchecks and balancesrule of law
